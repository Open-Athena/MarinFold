Can RL improve contacts-v1?

#208 ran eleven scored runs across five reward designs and none improved consensus R-precision. #237 changes one thing — the object the reward acts on — and gets the first checkpoints that do. Nine rewards, thirteen runs, 55 scored checkpoints.

The problem

A unit mismatch

#208's reward scored one rollout; the metric votes over 100 independent rollouts — an object no rollout can see. Making each rollout better made the hundred redundant.

The change

Put the candidates inside the sequence

Under <contacts-v1.multi> (#230) one rollout emits ~22 contact sets in a single generation. Reward that, and it acts on the kind of object the metric scores.

The result

It works, and not enough

The winner is M-K — reward = the rollout's own consensus, the deployed metric itself. At equal budget plain sampling still wins by 0.0090; at a larger one they draw level. RL closed the gap to ordinary sampling — it did not clearly open one.

Every reward helps at small KL and damages at large

What we learned

SCALE

E[r] = 0 is necessary and not sufficient. M-C's per-section reward is 366× larger at 1 section than at 22 — it paid the policy to emit a worse answer — while E[A] = 0 held exactly, because centring is computed inside the quantity being gamed.

GRADIENT

Every arm moved its candidate count where its own reward pointed. M-C's gradient is strongly negative (collapsed to 1.1 sections), M-B's strongly positive and self-paying (grew to ~26, held), M-F's nearly flat — and a weak gradient is not a safe one, it is an unconstrained one: M-F ran to 259 sections carrying 1.4 contacts each.

GATES

Criteria written against the failure you have seen will miss the next one. All three original gates are one-sided; M-F's failure pushed every one away from its threshold and none fired.

MATCH

The reward that won is the metric itself. M-K scores the rollout's own consensus and broadcasts one number — no per-section credit assignment, the mechanism the hypothesis was built on. Within-sequence credit assignment turned out not to be needed; what was needed was a reward computable on the object the metric scores. M-K is also the only arm neither killed by a gate nor diverged — it ran out of scheduled steps.

SHAPE

Crediting a section for what it ADDED is the one lever that moved a number. Every arm pays for candidates being individually good; none pays for them being different, and the metric is a vote that feeds on coverage (658 pairs per multi rollout vs 1,065 for 22 independent ones). A zero-sum within-rollout term raised ORACLE-best 0.5602 → 0.5677 at no cost to consensus, and shifted quality earlier in the rollout. Its first version died fastest of any arm here: “zero-sum” bounded the rollout’s level while leaving its shape decaying in section index — a stop-early signal, because a section owns the token that opens it.

PEAK

Long trajectories do not beat short ones. Three unrelated interventions — a 3× smaller learning rate, a 50× KL penalty, and a candidate-count floor — each slowed or reshaped the degradation past the peak. None produced a checkpoint better than the peak it started from. At matched distance, 3.3× the optimisation steps scored 0.0094 worse. Whatever bounds this is not the step size, not the distance per step, and not the candidate count.

SELECT

Selection is dominated by aggregation. An ORACLE selector of one draft reads 0.5646 where voting the same drafts reads 0.5750. A final section should synthesise its predecessors, not pick among them.
